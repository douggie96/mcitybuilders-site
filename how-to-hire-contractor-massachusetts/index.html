How to Hire a Contractor in Massachusetts — What the Project Involves

Massachusetts law requires any contractor performing home improvement work over $1,000 to hold a valid Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration with the Office of Consumer Affairs and Business Regulation (OCABR). You can verify any contractor's HIC number online at the OCABR website in under 60 seconds. A valid registration means the contractor has passed a background check and is bonded — critical protection if a project dispute goes to the Massachusetts Contractor Arbitration Program.

For structural work — additions, framing, load-bearing wall removal, deck construction — the contractor or their on-site supervisor must also hold a Construction Supervisor License (CSL) issued by the Massachusetts Department of Public Safety. Ask for both the HIC registration number and the CSL number before signing any contract. Beyond license verification, always get written bids, ask for references from projects completed in the past 12 months, and confirm in writing that the contractor pulls all required permits. Massachusetts How to Hire a Contractor FAQ

How do I verify a contractor's license in Massachusetts?

Go to the Massachusetts OCABR website and search the HIC registration database. For structural work, verify the CSL through the Massachusetts Department of Public Safety. Both searches are free and take under 60 seconds. Never hire a contractor who cannot provide their license number on request.

What is the difference between an HIC and a CSL in Massachusetts?

An HIC (Home Improvement Contractor) registration is required for any contractor doing home improvement work over $1,000. A CSL (Construction Supervisor License) is required for structural work — framing, load-bearing changes, additions, and deck construction. Some projects require only HIC; structural projects require both.

Should I get multiple bids before hiring a contractor in Massachusetts?

Yes — get at least two written bids for any project over $10,000. Compare scope, not just price. The lowest bid may exclude permit fees, disposal, or finishing work that another contractor includes. Ask each contractor to itemize their bid so you are comparing the same scope of work.

Who is responsible for pulling permits — me or the contractor?

The contractor is typically responsible for pulling permits in Massachusetts, and any reputable contractor will do so as part of their standard process. Confirm this in writing before signing. A contractor who asks you to pull your own permits or suggests skipping permits is a red flag — it shifts liability to you.

What are some red flags when hiring a general contractor?

The clearest red flags in Massachusetts are: no HIC registration number offered when you ask for it; a verbal quote with no written contract on a job over $1,000, which violates M.G.L. c. 142A; a demand for more than one-third of the contract price as a deposit, which the same statute prohibits; a request to be paid in cash or to a personal name rather than the registered business; no certificate of general liability and workers' compensation insurance naming you as certificate holder; an unsolicited door knock after a storm; pressure to sign today for a "discount"; and any suggestion that the job does not need a permit without the local building department being called. One more that homeowners miss: a contractor who will not put a completion date in the contract. Massachusetts requires start and substantial completion dates in writing.

How do I check if a contractor is licensed in Massachusetts?

There are two separate credentials and you should check both. HIC registration is issued by the Office of Consumer Affairs and Business Regulation and is searchable by business name, individual name, city, or registration number through the state's Home Improvement Contractor lookup on mass.gov. The Construction Supervisor License is issued by the Department of Public Safety and is searchable through the DPS license lookup. Ask for the numbers in writing before you search, then confirm the name on the registration matches the name on the contract exactly — a common problem is a salesperson whose company's registration is held under a different entity. Also confirm the registration status reads current and not expired, suspended, or revoked, and check whether the contractor has complaint history on file. Both searches are free and take about a minute.

Should I pay a contractor a deposit up front?

A deposit is normal, but Massachusetts law caps it. Under M.G.L. c. 142A, a registered home improvement contractor may not require an advance deposit of more than one-third of the total contract price — the only exception is the actual cost of special-order materials that must be purchased ahead, and that amount has to be documented in the contract. Anything beyond that is a violation, not a negotiating position. The balance should be tied to milestones you can see: rough framing complete, inspection passed, materials delivered, substantial completion. Never fund the entire job before work starts, never pay for materials that have not been delivered to your property, and pay by check or card so there is a record. What should be in a home improvement contract in Massachusetts?

For any residential home improvement job over $1,000, Massachusetts requires a written contract signed by both parties, with a copy given to the homeowner. It must include the contractor's name, address, telephone number, and HIC registration number; the full address of the work; a detailed description of the work and the materials to be used; the total contract price and a payment schedule including any deposit; the start date and the date of substantial completion; a statement of which party is responsible for obtaining permits, with the required notice that a homeowner who pulls their own permit forfeits access to the Guaranty Fund; the three-day right of cancellation; and a signature line for the arbitration clause. Add a written change-order process — every scope change priced and signed before the work happens — and a warranty term. If a document handed to you is missing the registration number, the completion date, or the payment schedule, it is not a compliant Massachusetts contract.

How do I know if a contractor's quote is too good to be true?

Get at least two or three written bids and compare the scope line by line, not the bottom number. A bid that comes in 25% or more below the others is almost always missing something: the permit and inspection fees, dumpster and disposal, demolition, subfloor or substrate repair, electrical or plumbing rough-in, paint and finish carpentry, or an allowance for fixtures set so low that the real selections trigger change orders later. Ask each contractor to itemize materials versus labor and to state their allowances in dollars. Two other tells — the low bidder cannot produce a current certificate of insurance (they may be carrying no workers' compensation, which puts liability for an injury on your homeowner's policy), and the low bidder will not give you references from jobs finished in the past twelve months in your area. Cheap quotes usually finish at the same price as the honest ones, just with more arguments along the way.

What happens if a contractor doesn't finish the job in Massachusetts?

Start with written notice to the contractor citing the completion date in your contract and giving a specific deadline to resume. If that fails, Massachusetts gives homeowners two remedies that most states do not. First, the Home Improvement Contractor Arbitration Program run by the Office of Consumer Affairs and Business Regulation — a binding, lower-cost alternative to court for disputes with a registered contractor. Second, the Guaranty Fund, which can reimburse a homeowner for actual losses up to $10,000 per claim after an unsatisfied judgment or arbitration award against a registered contractor. Both are only available if the contractor was registered and if you did not pull the building permit yourself — which is exactly why the "you pull the permit" request is such a serious red flag. You can also file a complaint with OCABR and, for structural work, with the Department of Public Safety against the CSL holder. Keep every contract, change order, photo, text, and canceled check; documentation is what decides these cases.
