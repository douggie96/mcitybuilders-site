Clinton Housing Stock — What We Run Into

Most of Clinton's housing stock dates from the town's mill era, roughly the 1890s through the 1920s. The neighborhoods around High Street, Water Street, Church Street, and down toward the Nashua River are full of two- and three-family homes built to house factory workers: balloon-framed, tall and narrow, with front porches stacked two or three high, steep stairwells, and kitchens at the back of each floor. Plenty of them still have the original layouts, along with knob-and-tube remnants, galvanized supply lines, and cast-iron drain stacks.

That era of construction is solid but it has known weak points. Balloon framing lets fire and air move between floors, so remodels often need fire blocking added. Stacked porches rot from the top down where the roof flashing failed decades ago, and by the time the bottom porch looks bad, the framing above is usually worse. Rear ells and additions were often built on shallow footings and have settled. None of that is a reason to walk away from a Clinton house. It is a reason to hire someone who has seen it before.

Clinton also has plenty of single-family capes and ranches from the 1940s through the 1960s on the outer streets, plus a smaller number of newer homes near the Berlin and Boylston lines. Those are more straightforward projects, but they are reaching the age where kitchens, baths, and decks are due for a full redo rather than another patch.

What a General Contractor Does vs. Hiring Trades Separately

In a Clinton three-family, hiring trades separately gets complicated fast. You need a carpenter to open walls, an electrician to pull out old wiring and bring circuits up to code, a plumber to replace a drain stack that serves all three units, a drywaller, a tile setter, a painter, and someone to coordinate all of it around tenants who still need a working bathroom. If you are your own general contractor, every one of those handoffs is yours to manage, and every gap in the schedule is a day a unit sits empty or a tenant is inconvenienced.

A general contractor takes that on. MCB writes one contract for the whole scope, sequences the trades so the plumber and electrician are in the wall at the same time instead of a week apart, pulls the permits through the Clinton Building Department under our own licenses, and schedules inspections so the wall does not get closed up before the inspector has seen it. Our own crew does the carpentry, tile, flooring, trim, and paint. Licensed subs handle the plumbing and electrical rough-in and finals.

The other difference is accountability. When one company owns the job, there is no argument about whose fault a problem is. If the floor is not level or the tile cracks, you call us and we fix it. That is what you are paying a general contractor for, and it is worth more in a dense, older, occupied building than almost anywhere else.
