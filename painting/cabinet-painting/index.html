Cabinet painting in Central Massachusetts

Last reviewed 2026-09-11

Painting kitchen cabinets is the cheapest way to change a kitchen and the easiest way to waste money. The difference is entirely in the preparation and the coating, and almost none of it is visible when the job is finished — which is exactly why it goes wrong so often.

We do this work as part of kitchen remodels and as a standalone job, across Lancaster, Clinton, Sterling, Worcester and the surrounding towns.

When painting your cabinets is the right call

Paint the boxes and doors you already have when:

  • The boxes are solid. Plywood or solid-wood carcasses in square, with doors that still close properly and hinges that are not torn out of the frame.
  • You like the layout. Paint changes colour. It does not move a sink, widen an aisle, or fix a corner cabinet you cannot reach into.
  • The doors are a paintable material. Solid wood, MDF and most factory-finished doors take paint well.

When we will tell you not to

We would rather lose the job than take your money for a finish that fails:

  • Thermofoil doors that are peeling. The vinyl skin lifts off the MDF underneath. Paint does not stop it and does not stick to it. These need replacing.
  • Water-damaged bases under a sink. Swollen, delaminating particle board will keep moving under the coating.
  • Boxes that are out of square or failing at the joints. You are paying to paint a cabinet that needs rebuilding.
  • A layout you already dislike. If the kitchen does not work, a colour change will not fix it and you will spend the money twice.

In those cases we will price the replacement or the partial rebuild instead, and tell you plainly which parts are worth keeping.

How the work is actually done

The sequence is the job. Skipping any of it is how cabinet paint ends up chipping off the drawer fronts within a year.

1. Strip the kitchen. Doors and drawer fronts come off and get labelled so every one goes back to the opening it came from. Hinges, pulls and catches are bagged by location. Nothing gets painted in place that can come off.

2. Degrease. Kitchen cabinets carry a film of cooking oil, especially above the range and around the handles. This is the step most often skipped and the single most common cause of failure. Everything gets washed with a degreaser and rinsed.

3. Sand and de-gloss. Existing finishes get scuffed so the primer has something mechanical to hold onto. Factory conversion varnish is hard and glossy, and paint will not bond to it on its own.

4. Repair. Dents, gouges, old hardware holes that are not being reused, and any grain that will telegraph through the finish all get filled and sanded flat.

5. Prime. A bonding primer, chosen for what the doors are made of. Oak and other open-grain woods get extra attention here, because the grain will read through a smooth finish unless it is filled and blocked.

6. Finish coats. Two coats of a cabinet-grade coating, sanded lightly between them. We spray doors and drawer fronts flat and horizontal — that is how you get a finish without brush marks or sag. Boxes and face frames are sprayed in place with everything masked, or brushed and rolled where spraying is not practical.

7. Cure before reassembly. Cabinet coatings reach full hardness days after they are dry to the touch. Doors that go back on too early stick to the frames and pull the finish off when opened. We wait.

8. Rehang and adjust. Doors back on their original openings, hinges adjusted so the reveals line up, new hardware installed if you are changing it.

The coating matters more than the colour

Wall paint on cabinets stays soft. It will mark with a fingernail and it will not survive the daily contact a kitchen gets. Cabinet work needs a coating formulated to cure hard — waterborne alkyds and dedicated cabinet enamels are the normal choices, and they behave differently from wall paint in how they flow, how long they take to harden, and how they need to be applied.

This is also why the drying schedule is not negotiable. A coating that says “recoat in four hours” may still need several days before it will take daily use.

Cost, honestly

Cabinet painting is priced by door and drawer count, not by kitchen square footage, because the doors are where the labour is. The variables that move the number:

  • How many doors and drawer fronts
  • Whether the boxes are being done as well as the doors
  • Open-grain wood such as oak, which needs grain filling to look smooth
  • Whether we spray on site or take the doors to a shop
  • Colour changes that need extra coverage — a dark stain going to white is more work than white going to grey
  • New hardware, and whether new holes have to be drilled and old ones filled

As a rough frame, painting cabinets typically lands somewhere between a fifth and a third of the cost of replacing them with new stock cabinetry. The gap widens the more doors you have.

We give you a written scope with the count and the coating named in it before any work starts.

How long your kitchen is out of action

For a typical kitchen, plan on the better part of a week, and longer if the boxes are being done as well. You will have the openings but not the doors for most of it. We talk through how to stage that before we start — most people empty the upper cabinets and live out of the lowers.

What we will not tell you

That it is a weekend job. That it will look like a factory finish if we brush it. That it will be indistinguishable from new cabinetry. A properly done cabinet repaint looks excellent and it is a genuinely good use of money — but the honest version is that you are refinishing what you have, not replacing it.
