Drywall repair and painting

Typical Central MA range: $350–$900 per typical patch-and-paint visit (typical Central MA range)

Holes, nail pops, cracks and ceiling stains patched, primed and painted so the repair disappears.

Book a free in-home consultation

The repair is easy. Making it disappear is the job.

Patching a hole in drywall takes minutes. Making that patch invisible once the wall is painted takes a lot more: the compound has to be feathered wide and sanded flat, the patch has to be primed so it does not flash, the texture has to match, and the paint has to go corner to corner. Most “we can see where it was fixed” complaints come down to skipping one of those steps.

This page is about that combined job: repairing damaged drywall and painting it so the room looks untouched. It suits the list of small repairs that builds up in every house, damage left behind after a plumber or electrician opens a wall, a ceiling stain after a leak is fixed, or a wall of nail pops and cracks before a room gets repainted. For a full room or whole-house repaint, see interior painting. If the damage came from taking down old paper, see wallpaper removal; if the trim needs attention too, see trim, door and stair painting. Plaster walls are a different material; our guide to painting older homes covers plaster cracks and loose plaster.

Who it is right for, and where it is not enough

Drywall repair and paint is right when the damage is on the surface: holes, dents, torn paper, nail pops, cracks at seams and corners, and stains from a leak that has been fixed.

It is not enough when something behind the wall is still wrong:

  • Active moisture. A stain that grows, drywall that feels soft, or a musty smell means water is still getting in. Fix the source first. For real water damage, see water damage restoration.
  • Cracks that keep widening or run diagonally from door and window corners across a whole wall can point to structural movement. Patching hides the symptom.
  • Large areas of mold are a remediation job, not a patch.

How the job runs, step by step

A typical scope looks like this:

  1. Walk and mark. We go room by room with you, mark every repair and agree how far the repaint goes, so there are no surprises.
  2. Protect. Floors covered, furniture moved and wrapped, dust controlled at the work area.
  3. Cut back damage. Loose paper, crumbled edges and soft drywall are cut out to solid material. Larger holes get a backing or a new piece screwed to framing.
  4. Tape and fill. Seams and cracks get tape, then two or three coats of compound, each feathered wider than the last. Nail pops get a screw set beside them.
  5. Sand and check. Sanded flat and checked with a work light held low against the wall, which shows ridges that overhead light hides.
  6. Match texture. Knockdown and orange-peel ceilings from the 1970s and 80s get re-textured to match before paint.
  7. Prime. Every patch gets primer so it does not flash. Water stains get a stain-blocking primer.
  8. Paint. Two finish coats, corner to corner on each repaired wall or ceiling.

Timeline: a list of small repairs in two or three rooms is usually two to three days, because compound needs drying time between coats. A replaced ceiling section is typically three to four days.

Choosing the paint

Sheen does more to hide a repair than anything else. Flat and matte finishes hide patches and wavy drywall; eggshell and satin show them. Ceilings should almost always be flat.

  • Benjamin Moore Regal Select and Sherwin-Williams Emerald Interior are our usual wall paints: washable, good coverage, and available in matte finishes that still clean up.
  • C2 LUXE covers walls in C2’s full color range, useful if you have picked C2 colors elsewhere in the house.
  • Fine Paints of Europe Eurolux is a waterborne acrylic wall paint in flat, matte and satin, used when a homeowner wants the deep color that brand is known for.
  • Farrow & Ball Estate Emulsion is a 2% sheen, chalky-looking finish that is wipeable but not washable, so it belongs in bedrooms and dining rooms. For kitchens, baths and hallways, Farrow & Ball’s Modern Emulsion is the washable option.

Whatever the brand, the patch needs primer first. Paint alone over bare compound almost always flashes.

New England issues

  • Seasonal movement. Dry winter heat shrinks framing, so nail pops and seam cracks show up most in late winter. Repairs made in February hold better if the screws go in properly, not just more compound.
  • Ice dams and leaks. Brown rings on upstairs ceilings in Holden, Sterling and Groton capes often come from ice dams. Wait until the leak is fixed and the drywall has dried before painting, or the stain comes back.
  • Older ranches and capes. Many 1950s–70s ranches in Leominster, Fitchburg and Clinton have textured ceilings. In homes built before the 1980s, joint compound and ceiling texture can contain asbestos, so we test suspect material before we cut or sand it. If it tests positive, removal is a job for a specialist asbestos contractor working under MassDEP rules.
  • Lead. Sanding painted walls or trim in a pre-1978 home can disturb lead paint, and that work falls under EPA RRP and Massachusetts lead-safe rules. Ask any contractor for their Massachusetts lead-safe renovation credentials in writing before work that disturbs paint in a pre-1978 home.

What to ask any contractor

  • Will you prime the patches, or paint straight over compound?
  • How far will the repaint extend: the patch, the wall, or the room?
  • How will you match the ceiling or wall texture?
  • Where did the water stain come from, and has that been fixed?
  • For older homes: will suspect texture or compound be tested first?

If you have a list of repairs, book a free in-home consultation. We walk every room with you, mark each repair, show paint samples, and send a written estimate that spells out what gets fixed and how far the painting goes.

Common questions

Why can I still see the patch after it was painted?

Usually flashing. Joint compound absorbs paint differently from the painted wall around it, so the patch dries with a different sheen. Priming the patch and repainting the wall corner to corner fixes it. Touching up only the patch almost never blends.

Do you have to repaint the whole wall for one hole?

To make it invisible, yes, in most cases. Paint fades and sheen changes over time, so even the same can of paint rarely matches a wall that is a few years old. Repainting to the nearest corners is the standard way to hide a repair.

My ceiling has a brown stain from a leak. Can you just paint it?

Only after the leak is fixed and the drywall is dry and solid. Water stains bleed through ordinary paint, so the spot needs a stain-blocking primer first. If the drywall is sagging or soft, that section is cut out and replaced.

Why do nail pops keep coming back?

Framing lumber shrinks and moves as the house dries out each heating season, pushing nails through the finish. Setting a screw beside the nail, then re-finishing, holds better than tapping the nail back in.

Is it safe to sand old drywall or ceiling texture?

In homes built before the 1980s, joint compound and textured ceilings can contain asbestos, and painted surfaces in pre-1978 homes can contain lead. Have suspect material tested before sanding or demolition, and ask any contractor for their Massachusetts lead-safe renovation credentials in writing before work that disturbs paint in a pre-1978 home.
