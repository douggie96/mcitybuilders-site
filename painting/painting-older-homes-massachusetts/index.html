Painting older homes in Massachusetts

Last reviewed 2026-09-11

Most of the housing stock around Lancaster, Clinton, Sterling and Worcester predates 1978. That single fact changes how a painting job has to be planned, priced and carried out — and it is the reason a quote for an 1890 farmhouse and a quote for a 2015 colonial are not comparable.

This page is about what is actually different. If you own an older home in Central Massachusetts, this is the stuff worth knowing before you get quotes.

Lead paint: the part nobody should be casual about

Homes built before 1978 may contain lead-based paint. In Massachusetts this is not a footnote — it is a regulated area with real legal weight, and it is where the cheapest quote is most likely to be the most dangerous one.

What the rules actually say. Under the federal Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule, contractors doing work that disturbs painted surfaces in pre-1978 housing are required to be certified and to use lead-safe work practices. Massachusetts also has its own Lead Law, which places specific obligations on owners of homes where a child under six lives.

What lead-safe work looks like in practice. Containment of the work area. No open-flame burning or uncontrolled power sanding of suspect surfaces. HEPA vacuums rather than a shop vac. Plastic down, edges sealed, controlled cleanup, and waste handled properly rather than swept into a contractor bag.

What this means for your quote. A painter who quotes an 1890 house exactly as they would quote a new build either has not thought about it or is not planning to do it properly. The containment and cleanup are real hours and they belong in the price.

Before you hire anyone for a pre-1978 home, ask to see their lead certification, and ask them to describe their containment. If the answer is vague, keep looking. This applies to us as much as to anyone else — ask us the same question.

If a full deleading is what you actually need, that is a separate regulated trade with its own certification, and a different scope from painting. We will tell you when that is the conversation you are having rather than quoting around it.

Plaster, not drywall

Older homes in this area are plaster over wood lath, and plaster does not behave like drywall.

  • Hairline cracking is normal and usually seasonal — the house moves. It can be filled and it will often come back if the underlying movement is not addressed.
  • Cracks that step or widen are telling you something structural. Painting over them hides a symptom.
  • Loose or “drummy” plaster has separated from the lath. Pressing it moves it. Paint will not reattach it. It needs re-anchoring or cutting out and patching before any finish goes on.
  • Old repairs done in joint compound over plaster often flash differently under new paint and need spot priming.

Good prep on plaster is most of the job. The painting itself is quick by comparison.

Trim that has been painted a dozen times

Original trim in a 120-year-old house may be carrying a very thick paint history — layers that have softened detail, filled profiles, and cracked into alligatoring in places.

The honest options are a spectrum:

  • Scuff, prime and repaint. Cheapest. Keeps the built-up texture.
  • Spot-strip the failed areas, feather the edges, rebuild the surface, then paint. Middle ground and usually the right answer.
  • Full strip back to wood. Expensive, slow, and on pre-1978 trim it is a lead-safe operation with real constraints on method. Worth it when the trim profile is exceptional and currently unreadable.

We will tell you which of those the trim in front of us actually justifies.

Exterior paint that is failing, and why

Exterior coatings on older homes usually fail for a reason that is not the paint.

  • Peeling in sheets down to bare wood is often moisture pushing from inside — a bathroom without working ventilation, or a wall cavity without a vapour barrier.
  • Peeling concentrated under a window or below the eaves is usually water getting in at the flashing or the trim.
  • Alligatoring is a coating that has become too thick and too brittle over decades.
  • Chalking is a coating at the end of its life; it powders off on your hand.

Repainting over an active moisture problem buys you two years at best. On older homes we look for the source first, because we also do the rot repair and envelope work that fixes it — and finding it before you pay for paint is cheaper for you than finding it afterwards.

The Massachusetts painting calendar

Exterior painting here has a real weather window. Most exterior coatings need surface temperatures above roughly 50°F, and they need them overnight as well as during the day — the surface has to stay warm enough for the film to form properly as it cures.

In practice that means late spring through early autumn for exterior work in Central Massachusetts, with the shoulder weeks depending on the product and the forecast. Autumn work also has to contend with heavy dew, which can ruin a fresh coat overnight.

Interior painting runs year round, which is why a lot of homeowners here move interior work into the winter months and keep the summer free for the outside of the house.

What we do before we quote an older home

  • Walk the exterior and look for the water source behind any failing paint
  • Check the plaster for loose sections rather than just looking at it
  • Identify the age of the house and plan the work accordingly for pre-1978 stock
  • Look at what is behind the trim, not just the trim
  • Tell you if what you actually need is carpentry or envelope repair rather than paint

That last one matters. We are a general contractor, not only a painting company — so when the honest answer is “this is rot, not a paint problem”, we can say it without losing the job, and we can fix it.
