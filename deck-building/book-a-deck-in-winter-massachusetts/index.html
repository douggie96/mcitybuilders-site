Should You Book a Deck in Winter for Spring in Massachusetts?

Last reviewed 2026-10-13

Yes. If you want a new deck or a deck rebuild ready for summer in Central Massachusetts, winter is when to design it, price it and get the town permit moving. The ground is frozen and footings have to go 48 inches deep, so most of the digging and concrete waits for the thaw. But the paperwork does not, and the homeowners who have plans and a permit in hand by spring are the ones who get built first.

We are a Lancaster-based general contractor building decks across Worcester County, the Nashoba Valley and MetroWest. This page is about timing: what to do in the cold months, what can and cannot be built in a Massachusetts winter, and how the permit side works. For prices and materials, see our deck building page and our deck cost guide.

Why winter is the right time to plan a deck

Think of a deck as two jobs. One is planning: measuring, design, material choice, a written price and a permit. The other is building: holes, concrete, framing, boards, rails and stairs. Only the second job needs good weather.

Here is why the planning job belongs in winter:

  • Spring fills up fast. Our guide to planning spring projects early puts it plainly: construction picks up in Massachusetts in April, and by May the busiest contractors are fully booked through the summer. If you call when the snow melts, you are calling with everyone else.
  • The permit takes time, and it is out of anyone’s hands. Every town reviews plans on its own schedule. Bedford’s building department, for example, says decks usually take 7 to 10 days to review. Other towns are slower, and spring is when their desks are busiest too.
  • You have time to choose the right material. Pressure-treated, cedar, composite and PVC each cost and age differently. Winter gives you weeks, not days, to look at samples and run the numbers.
  • You get a fixed price before you commit. Our quotes are fixed prices in writing, and every range we publish includes the permit, the footings and the town inspections. You can read the number at your kitchen table in January instead of rushing a decision in May.

From first call to final inspection, our deck cost guide puts a typical project at 6 to 10 weeks. Once the permit is issued, the on-site build for a typical 300 to 400 square foot deck runs about 5 to 10 working days. Most of the calendar is the steps before the build. Those are the steps winter is good for.

What can and cannot be built in a Massachusetts winter

Footings: the frost line sets the rules

Footings are the concrete piers that hold a deck up. In Massachusetts they have to go below the frost line, and the state’s residential code, 780 CMR 51.00, lists the frost line depth at 48 inches. We set deck footings 48 inches deep, and your local building department has the final word for your lot.

That matters in winter for a simple reason: ground that freezes and thaws pushes shallow footings up. Our deck repair and rebuilds page lists posts on precast blocks and heaved footings among the clearest signs a deck is failing.

Winter makes footings hard in two ways:

  1. Frozen ground. Digging a 48-inch hole through frozen topsoil is slow and costly, and the bottom of the hole has to be sound.
  2. Cold concrete. The American Concrete Institute’s cold-weather guide (ACI 306R-16) treats it as cold weather when the air temperature has fallen to, or is expected to fall below, 40°F during the protection period. Its guidance: do not place concrete on frozen ground, because thawing can cause settlement and cracking, and protect the concrete until it reaches at least 500 psi.

In Worcester, that is a normal winter. NOAA’s 1991–2020 climate normals put Worcester’s average January high at 32°F and the average low at 17°F.

So the honest answer is that footings are a thaw-season job most years. A mild stretch can open a window, but nobody should promise you a January pour.

Framing, decking and railings: cold is workable, with rules

Once the footings are in and inspected, the frame can go up in cold weather. Decking boards can too, but each material has its own cold-weather rules, and they are not optional:

  • Composite (Trex). Trex’s installation guide calls for a 3/16-inch end-to-end gap below 40°F and 1/8 inch above it, and a 1/2-inch gap where boards meet a wall or post in the cold versus 1/4 inch in warm weather. Trex’s international guide also says to lay cold-stored boards out on the framing, without cutting or fastening, so they acclimate to the current temperature first.
  • Composite (TimberTech). TimberTech’s guide calls for a 3/16-inch butt-joint gap at 32°F and below, 1/8 inch from 33°F to 74°F, and 1/32 inch at 75°F and above. It also requires pre-drilling below 45°F to avoid end splitting and screw shear, and it notes the boards may become slippery in winter weather.
  • PVC (AZEK and TimberTech Advanced PVC). PVC moves more with temperature than composite, which is why our PVC decks page warns that boards installed on a cold November day will grow in July. Cold-weather installs are one of the most common PVC mistakes we see. The fix is setting gaps from the gapping chart for the temperature on install day.
  • Pressure-treated. PT can be built in the cold, but it should not be stained until it is dry. Our pressure-treated decks page explains the test: the wood is ready when it soaks up water instead of beading it. Wet-treated lumber can take a few weeks to a few months to get there, and stain goes on a dry day within the product’s temperature range, not in late fall. See our deck and fence staining page for that step.

Rebuilds: it depends on what is under the boards

If your existing frame is sound, a resurface (new boards and railings on the old frame) skips the footing work entirely, so it is less tied to the thaw. If the footings are shallow blocks or the ledger was never flashed, you are looking at a real rebuild, and that brings back the same footing rules as a new deck. Our deck repair and rebuilds page walks through how to tell the difference.

Permits and inspections in Massachusetts towns

Do you need a permit?

Almost always. The state building code, 780 CMR 105.1, makes it unlawful to construct, reconstruct, alter or repair a building or structure without first filing an application with the building official and getting the required permit. In practice, any deck attached to the house or over 200 square feet needs a building permit. Structural work on an existing deck, such as a new ledger, joists, posts, footings, stairs or guards, usually needs one too. Swapping out a few boards usually does not.

What the town will ask for

Each town runs its own building department, but the paperwork looks similar:

  • A plot plan. Acton asks for a plot plan showing where the deck will go, and checks it against zoning, wetland and septic setbacks. Bedford lists decks among the projects that need a scaled plot plan and foundation plans.
  • Construction plans. Needham’s deck page asks for building plans showing footing size and depth, among other details.
  • Timing rules. Needham says that once the permit is issued, construction must begin within 6 months. A permit pulled in February or March comfortably covers a spring build.

The inspections

The town inspects the work at set points. Hubbardston’s building department says that for a deck attached to a home, the contractor must call for an inspection at the placement of the footings. Needham requires an excavation inspection before concrete is poured, a rough frame inspection, and a final building inspection. Bedford asks for 24 hours’ notice on every inspection.

We file the permit and handle every inspection. You do not.

A month-by-month plan, December to May

Weather sets the exact dates. This is the order things happen in, not a promise of specific days.

December: decide what you want. How will you use the deck: grilling, dining, a hot tub, a view? Measure the space, take photos of the back of the house, and find your plot plan or mortgage survey if you have one. Acton notes a mortgage survey plan may be acceptable depending on the setbacks, so find out early. Read our deck cost guide so the numbers do not surprise you.

January: site visit and material choice. We visit, take measurements and talk through materials. This is the month to compare boards side by side. Our published installed ranges run $34 to $55 per square foot for pressure-treated pine, $45 to $72 for western red cedar, $55 to $95 for capped composite and $70 to $115 for cellular PVC. Railings matter too. See deck railings and stairs for the code basics on guard heights and stairs.

February: lock the design and the price. Approve the layout and sign a written, fixed-price contract. We draw the structural plans and get the plot plan in order for the permit application.

March: permit filed and reviewed. We file with your town’s building department. Review time depends on the town and how busy it is. If the town asks for changes, there is still time to make them before the ground thaws.

April: footings. Once the frost is out of the ground and the forecast cooperates, the holes go in to 48 inches, the town inspects them, and concrete is poured on sound, unfrozen ground.

May: frame, boards, rails, final. Framing goes up and gets its inspection, then decking (gapped for the temperature that day), railings and stairs, then the final inspection and cleanup. If you chose pressure-treated, staining comes later in the season once the wood is dry.

Frequently asked questions

Can you build a deck in the winter in Massachusetts?

Parts of it, depending on the weather. Footings are the hard part: Massachusetts code puts the frost line at 48 inches, and ACI guidance says not to place concrete on frozen ground and to protect it from freezing until it reaches about 500 psi. Framing, decking and railings can go together in cold weather if the footings are in and the board maker's cold-weather gapping rules are followed. Design, estimating and the permit are the parts that fit winter best.

When should I call a deck builder if I want my deck done by summer?

In winter. Our guide to planning spring projects notes that construction picks up in Massachusetts in April, and by May the busiest contractors are fully booked through the summer. Calling in December, January or February leaves time for the site visit, design, a written price and the town permit before the ground thaws.

Do I need a building permit for a deck in Massachusetts?

Usually, yes. The state building code, 780 CMR, makes it unlawful to construct or alter a structure without first applying to the building official and getting the required permit. Any deck attached to the house or over 200 square feet needs one. Towns such as Acton, Bedford and Needham ask for a plot plan showing where the deck goes, and we pull the permit and handle the inspections.

How deep do deck footings have to be in Central Massachusetts?

Below the frost line. The Massachusetts Residential Code lists a frost line depth of 48 inches, and we set deck footings 48 inches deep. Your local building department has the final word for your lot, and towns such as Needham and Hubbardston inspect the footings before concrete goes in.

Is it OK to install composite or PVC decking when it is cold?

It can be, but the gaps change. Trex calls for a 3/16-inch end-to-end gap below 40°F versus 1/8 inch above it. TimberTech calls for a 3/16-inch butt-joint gap at 32°F and below and requires pre-drilling below 45°F. PVC moves the most: boards installed on a cold November day will grow in July, so the gapping chart for the install-day temperature has to be followed.

Does booking in winter change what my deck costs?

We do not run winter pricing. What booking early buys you is time: time to compare materials, a fixed written price before you commit, and a permit in hand before the spring rush. For ranges, most decks we build in Worcester County land between $8,000 and $55,000, and our deck cost guide breaks that down by material and size.
