Usually, yes. In Massachusetts, any bathroom remodel that touches plumbing, wiring or framing needs at least one permit, and a full remodel typically needs three: building, plumbing and electrical. Purely cosmetic work like painting, new tile or a vanity swapped in the same spot often needs no building permit at all.

## The three permits a Massachusetts bathroom can need

**Building permit.** This falls under the Massachusetts State Building Code, 780 CMR. Homes are covered by the 10th edition residential code, based on the 2021 International Residential Code with Massachusetts amendments and the only edition in force since July 1, 2025. Painting, tiling, carpeting, cabinets and countertops are generally exempt. Moving walls, framing a new shower or cutting into floor joists is not.

**Plumbing and gas permit.** The state plumbing code, 248 CMR, is stricter than most. Plumbing and gas work must be done by a Massachusetts master or journeyman plumber, who pulls this permit in their own name. Homeowners generally cannot do their own plumbing here, even in their own house.

**Electrical permit.** The electrician pulls this one. It covers GFCI protection on the outlets, new or upgraded circuits, lighting, heated floors and the exhaust fan wiring.

## Which bathroom projects need which permits

| Project | Building | Plumbing | Electrical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paint, new mirror, new hardware | No | No | No |
| Re-tile walls or floor, same layout | Usually no | No | No |
| Vanity or toilet replaced in the same spot | Usually no | Often yes, varies by town | No |
| New exhaust fan or lighting | Sometimes | No | Yes |
| Tub-to-shower conversion | Often | Yes | Usually |
| Full gut remodel | Usually | Yes | Yes |
| Layout change, moving the toilet or shower | Yes | Yes | Yes |

Like-for-like fixture swaps are where towns differ most. Some treat a toilet swap as repair work; others want a plumbing permit any time a fixture comes off. Once you move a toilet, relocate a drain or take out a wall, there is no gray area: building, plumbing and electrical permits, every time.

One item inspectors check closely is the exhaust fan. It has to be ducted to the outdoors, not into the attic. Fans vented into the attic cause mold on the attic sheathing. ## Who should pull the permit

Your contractor should pull the building permit, and the plumber and electrician pull their own. In Massachusetts, if you pull the building permit yourself while hiring someone to do the work, you generally lose access to the state Guaranty Fund, which can reimburse homeowners when a registered contractor fails to finish or does bad work. If a contractor asks you to pull the permit "to save time," treat it as a warning sign. ## Inspections you should expect

A permitted bathroom is inspected in stages, and walls cannot close until the rough work passes:

- **Rough plumbing**: drain, vent and supply lines, usually with a pressure or water test.
- **Rough electrical**: wiring, boxes and circuits, before insulation and drywall.
- **Framing**: when walls move or a new shower is framed. Not every town requires it.
- **Final**: building, plumbing and electrical once the room is finished.

## What permits cost and how they affect your timeline

Your town sets the fee, usually based on project value. For most bathroom projects, permit fees run **$100 to $500** in total. Larger primary-suite projects run **$500 to $1,200**. A few local examples: Clinton charges $12 per $1,000 of construction value with a $60 minimum, and Sterling has a $75 minimum on residential permits. Concord takes applications online through the PermitEyes portal, run by the Concord Building Division at 141 Keyes Road. Our [guide to building permits in Central Massachusetts](/building-permits-central-massachusetts) has more town-by-town detail.

Typical Massachusetts project ranges:

| Project | Typical cost | Typical time on site |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmetic refresh or powder room | $8,000–$16,000 | About a week or less |
| Tub-to-shower conversion | $8,000–$18,000 | 2–3 weeks |
| Hall bath remodel | $16,000–$28,000 | 2–3 weeks |
| Full remodel with layout change | $32,000–$60,000 | 3–5 weeks |
| Primary suite | $70,000–$125,000 | Varies with scope |

When plumbing or electrical is involved, permit processing can add **3 to 5 weeks** before demolition starts. The full breakdown is in our [Massachusetts bathroom remodel cost guide](/bathroom-remodel-cost-massachusetts).

## What happens if the work is not permitted

Skipping the permit rarely saves money. Most towns charge double the normal fee to permit work after the fact, and the inspector can ask you to open finished walls to see the plumbing and wiring. The bigger cost shows up when you sell: buyers' inspectors and attorneys check permit history, and an unpermitted bathroom can mean a lower offer, a delayed closing, or a demand that you permit it before the sale.
