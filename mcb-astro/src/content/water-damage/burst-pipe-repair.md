## What to do in the first hour

Work through this list in order.

1. **Shut off the main water valve.** Stopping the water comes first. A split supply line keeps running at full pressure until someone closes the valve, and every minute it runs puts more water into the walls, ceilings and floors below. Utah State University Extension advises that the adults and older children in a house should all know where the main water shutoff is. If you have never looked for yours, do it before winter.
2. **Stay away from wet electrical.** Ready.gov warns not to touch electrical equipment if it is wet or if you are standing in water, and to turn off the electricity to prevent shock only if it is safe to do so. If the breaker panel is in a wet basement, do not wade in. Call your utility or an electrician.
3. **Call a plumber.** The pipe has to be cut out and repaired before anything else happens. If other pipes are frozen but not yet split, the Red Cross advises against using a blowtorch, kerosene or propane heater, or any open flame to thaw them.
4. **Call your insurance company.** The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) points out that most insurers have deadlines for reporting claims, so contact your agent or company as soon as possible.
5. **Document everything before it gets moved.** NAIC's guidance is to take photos or videos and make a list of the damage, keep every receipt, and save damaged items so your insurer can inspect them.
6. **Get the dry-out started.** Your insurer may send its own mitigation vendor, or you may call one directly. Either way, the clock on mold is already running.

## Why pipes burst in Massachusetts homes

Water expands when it freezes. The Red Cross puts it plainly: that expansion puts pressure on whatever is holding the water, including pipes. What surprises most people is *where* the pipe actually splits. As Live Science explains, the ice plug pushes liquid water toward the closed faucet, pressure builds between the ice and the faucet, and the pipe usually ruptures at a spot where there is little or no ice. So the split can be several feet away from the cold spot that caused it, often inside a finished wall or ceiling.

Live Science puts the typical danger point at about 20°F. In Central Massachusetts, that is not a cold snap. That is a normal January night. NOAA's 1991–2020 climate normals put Worcester's average January low at 17°F, against a high of 32°F. Boston averages 23°F.

### Where pipes freeze in New England houses

The Red Cross and Live Science list the same trouble spots, and they match what we open up after a loss:

- **Pipes in exterior walls** with little or no insulation, especially kitchen sink lines on an outside wall and second-floor bathroom supply lines.
- **Unheated spaces:** basements, crawlspaces, attics and garages, which the Red Cross names specifically for supply pipes in unheated interior areas.
- **Outdoor hose bibs (sill cocks)** and the short run of pipe feeding them through the sill.
- **Under-sink cabinets** on outside walls, where cabinet doors trap cold air against the pipes.
- **Three-season rooms, additions and cottages** with thin walls and little insulation, which Live Science specifically calls out as at risk.

Additions and older layouts often run plumbing through spaces that were never meant to be heated, which is why a house can go years without trouble and then split a line in one long cold stretch.

## What gets damaged and what has to be rebuilt

The plumber's repair is usually the small part of the bill. Angi puts the national average cost to repair a burst pipe at about $500, with most repairs between $200 and $3,000 for the plumbing work alone. The bigger cost is everything the water touched on its way down.

Here is what a typical burst-pipe loss leaves behind:

- **Drywall and ceilings.** The EPA's water-damage guidance says wallboard may be dried in place only if there is no obvious swelling and the seams are intact. If not, it gets removed, discarded and replaced.
- **Insulation.** The same EPA guidance says wet fiberglass and cellulose insulation should be discarded and replaced, not dried.
- **Flooring and subfloor.** Hardwood cups and buckles, laminate swells at the seams, and carpet pad holds water. Subfloor under a long-running leak may need to come out too.
- **Cabinets and countertops.** Particleboard cabinet boxes and toe kicks soak up water and swell.
- **Trim, doors and paint.** Baseboard, casing and door jambs take on water first because they sit at floor level.
- **Bathrooms and kitchens.** When the leak is inside a bathroom wall or under a kitchen, the rebuild can mean tile, a vanity, a tub surround, or a section of cabinets and counters.

For the dry-out itself, HomeAdvisor reports that water damage restoration typically runs $1,383 to $6,369, with an average of $3,868, or roughly $3.50 to $7.50 per square foot depending on the category of water. Those are national ranges from HomeAdvisor, not our pricing. Reconstruction is priced separately against your actual scope.

## Dry-out first, then rebuild: how it works with your insurer and mitigation company

A burst-pipe loss has two separate phases, done by two different kinds of companies.

**Phase 1 – Mitigation (the dry-out).** A water-mitigation company extracts standing water, removes wet materials that cannot be saved, and runs air movers and dehumidifiers until the structure reads dry. This is the emergency part, and it is not what we do.

**Phase 2 – Reconstruction (the rebuild).** Once the mitigation company confirms the structure is dry and the affected wall and floor cavities read within a normal moisture range, the rebuild starts. That is where we come in.

In between sits the insurance adjuster. The adjuster documents the loss and writes the scope of loss: the list of what will be repaired or replaced. We build to that documented scope and give you a written, fixed-price estimate that lines up with it. We coordinate with your insurer and your mitigation company so nobody is guessing about what was approved.

### A few things to know about insurance

Every policy is different, so check yours:

- **Sudden and accidental vs. gradual.** The NAIC explains that homeowners policies generally cover a sudden and accidental discharge from a plumbing system, but damage may not be covered if the insurer determines you neglected your plumbing by not taking preventive measures. A slow leak that went on for months is treated very differently from a pipe that split last night.
- **Heat and occupancy matter.** The Massachusetts Division of Insurance notes that most policies cover sudden and accidental water damage from a burst pipe only if the home was properly heated and not left unoccupied. The NAIC adds that if a home is vacant, the expectation is usually to shut off the water supply.
- **Get more than one bid.** The NAIC recommends getting more than one bid on repair projects and never paying cash or writing a check before the work is done.

Nothing here is legal or insurance advice. Your policy and your adjuster have the final word on coverage.

## Preventing it next winter

Once the house is put back together, the goal is to never do this again. These come from the Red Cross, the Massachusetts Division of Insurance, the NAIC and Utah State University Extension:

- **Keep the heat steady.** The Red Cross recommends keeping the thermostat at the same temperature day and night during cold weather. If you travel, leave the heat on and set no lower than 55°F.
- **Shut off and drain outside faucets.** The Division of Insurance recommends shutting off and covering exterior faucets used for garden hoses.
- **Insulate exposed pipes.** Pipe sleeves or UL-listed heat tape on lines in basements, crawlspaces, attics and garages, per the Red Cross.
- **Open the cabinet doors** under kitchen and bathroom sinks on cold nights so warm air reaches the plumbing.
- **Let a faucet drip** in very cold weather if it is fed by exposed pipes. Even a trickle helps.
- **Keep the garage door closed** if there are water lines in the garage.
- **Know where your main shutoff is.** USU Extension's advice is that the adults and older children in the house should all know where it is.
- **If the house will sit empty,** shut off the water supply, as the NAIC advises for vacant homes.
