## When a deck needs attention

Most decks don't fail all at once. They give warnings for years, and the warnings are easy to miss because the boards on top can look fine while the frame underneath rots. Here's what to look for, roughly in order of seriousness:

1. **The ledger.** Go under the deck and look where it meets the house. Is it bolted through, or only nailed? Is there metal or membrane flashing tucked behind the siding and over the ledger? Dark streaks and soft wood here are the most serious sign on any deck.
2. **Soft wood.** Push an awl or screwdriver into the tops of joists, the ends of beams and the base of posts. If it sinks in easily, that wood is rotting.
3. **Wobbly rail posts.** Lean hard on each post. Movement usually means a notched post, a twisting rim or rot at the bolts.
4. **Rusted hardware.** Flaking joist hangers and corroded bolts, especially on older decks built with plain steel.
5. **Posts and footings.** Posts set in the soil or on precast blocks, footings that have heaved, or posts leaning out of plumb.
6. **Stairs.** Stringers pulling away at the top, cracked treads, or a bottom step sitting on dirt.
7. **Missing lateral hardware.** Newer code details tie the deck back to the house framing to resist sideways movement. Older decks rarely have this.

If you find item one or two, don't wait for spring cleanup.

## Resurface or rebuild?

This is the real decision on most older decks, and it hinges on the frame, not the boards.

**Resurface** (new boards and railing on the existing frame) makes sense when the joists are solid top to bottom, the ledger is bolted and flashed or can be corrected, the footings are below frost depth, and the joist spacing matches what the new boards need. That's often true of decks built in the last 15 years.

**Rebuild** makes sense when the ledger was never flashed and the rim behind it is soft, the footings are shallow blocks, many joists are soft on top, or the frame spacing would need so many new joists that you're paying for a new frame anyway. Putting composite or PVC boards on a failing frame means paying twice.

**A middle path** we often recommend: keep the sound footings and beams, replace the ledger and joists, then deck and rail new. A typical scope looks like that on a 20-year-old deck whose footings pass but whose joist tops are gone.

## What deck repairs cost in Central MA

National guides put ledger replacement and joist repairs at a few hundred to a few thousand dollars, but they rarely include opening the wall, shoring the deck, or Massachusetts labor. These are typical ranges, not quotes:

| Typical scope | Typical installed range |
|---|---|
| Replace a small area of boards or stair treads | $500–$2,000 |
| Rebuild a rail section with reinforced posts | $1,000–$3,000 |
| Sister or replace a run of soft joists | $1,500–$5,000 |
| Resurface 16x20 on a sound frame, pressure-treated | $4,500–$6,500 |
| Resurface 16x20 on a sound frame, composite | $12,000–$17,000 |
| Tear-off and full rebuild with new ledger, flashing and footings | $9,000–$22,000 |

Repairs are the hardest deck work to price sight unseen, because rot rarely stops where it looks like it stops. Our written estimate itemises what we'll replace, what we'll check once it's open, how any hidden damage would be priced, and what's excluded.

## What the job involves

1. **Inspection.** We go under the deck, probe the frame, check the ledger, measure joist spacing and footing depth where visible.
2. **Permit.** Structural repairs are filed with the town.
3. **Shoring and demo.** Temporary posts carry the deck while the ledger or beams come out. Old treated wood is bagged and disposed of properly.
4. **House-side repair.** Siding off at the ledger, rim and sheathing checked and repaired, flashing installed.
5. **Structure, then surface.** New ledger, joists or posts, joist tape, then boards and railing.
6. **Inspection and cleanup.**

A ledger repair is usually two to four days. A resurface runs three to seven days. A full rebuild is similar to a new deck.

## New England issues for older decks

**Frost-heaved footings.** Decks built on precast blocks or shallow holes move every winter. Proper footings go below frost depth, commonly 48 inches in Central Massachusetts; confirm with your town.

**Older houses.** Triple-deckers and older colonials in Worcester, and the mill-era houses in Clinton, often have back decks and stairs added over decades by different hands. We expect to find mixed lumber, undersized joists and surprises at the house wall. The 1960s ranches around Hudson and Marlborough often have a ledger nailed straight through the siding.

**Lead paint.** Opening siding on a pre-1978 house disturbs old paint. EPA lead-safe rules apply to that work. Ask any contractor for their lead-safe certification in writing before it starts.

**Hidden water damage.** If rot has run into the sill or wall framing, it becomes a [water damage restoration](/water-damage-restoration) job, and sometimes a [building restoration](/building-restoration) job, before it's a deck job.

## What to ask any contractor about deck repair

- Did you go under the deck and probe the frame, or just look at the boards?
- How will you attach and flash the new ledger?
- How deep are my footings, and will you reuse them?
- How will hidden damage be priced if you find it?
- Will the repair be permitted and inspected?
