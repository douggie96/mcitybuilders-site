## Who a coated floor is right for, and where it fails

A coated concrete floor turns a dusty, stained, salt-eaten slab into a surface you can hose off. Every Massachusetts winter your car drags in slush loaded with salt and brine, and bare concrete soaks it up.

A coating is the right call for:

- **Attached and detached garages**, especially ones that double as a workshop, gym or storage space.
- **Unfinished basements** where you want a clean, bright floor for laundry, storage or a workbench area without adding anything that can rot or grow mold.
- **Utility rooms and mudroom slabs** that see water and grit.

Where it's the wrong pick:

- **A basement that floods or weeps water.** A coating seals the top of the slab; it doesn't stop water pushing up from below or through the walls. Fix the water first. If you've had water in the basement, our [water damage restoration](/water-damage-restoration) team can help sort that out.
- **A finished living space where you want warmth underfoot.** For a basement family room, [carpet](/flooring/carpet-installation) or [luxury vinyl plank](/flooring/luxury-vinyl-plank) is usually a better fit.
- **A badly heaved or broken slab.** A coating will just highlight big displaced sections. That slab needs repair first.

## Grind prep vs acid etch

Prep is where coatings live or die. There are two common ways to open up concrete so a coating can bite:

- **Acid etching** is what most DIY kits use. It's cheap, but it's inconsistent on a garage slab, doesn't remove oil, old paint or cure-and-seal products, and leaves residue if it isn't rinsed perfectly.
- **Diamond grinding** uses a floor grinder with dust extraction to take off the top layer of the slab, down to clean, open concrete. It removes old coatings and contamination and gives a consistent profile every time.

We grind. It's the main difference between a floor that holds up and one that peels at the tire tracks after the first winter.

## Epoxy vs polyaspartic, flake vs solid

**Epoxy** builds thickness well and bonds hard to properly prepared concrete. It's a strong base coat. On its own as a topcoat it can yellow in sunlight and takes days to cure hard enough for cars.

**Polyaspartic** cures quickly, holds its color in UV, and resists scratches and chemicals well. It costs more, and its short working time means it has to be applied by someone who's done it before.

Many solid systems pair them: epoxy base, polyaspartic top.

**Flake vs solid color.** A full flake broadcast hides dust, tire marks and small imperfections in the slab, and the texture adds grip when the floor is wet. Solid color looks clean and works well in a basement, but it shows everything and gets slick when wet unless an anti-slip additive goes in the topcoat. For a garage, most people pick flake.

## What the job involves, step by step

A typical scope looks like this for a two-car garage:

1. **Clear and inspect.** The garage gets emptied and we look at cracks, spalling, old coatings and oil stains.
2. **Moisture test.** We test the slab before we commit to a system (see below).
3. **Grind.** Diamond grind the whole floor with dust extraction, edges included.
4. **Repair.** Fill cracks, spalls and pitting with a repair compound, then grind it flush. Control joints get handled as agreed.
5. **Base coat.** Apply the primer or base coat. On a slab with high moisture readings, this is a moisture-mitigating primer.
6. **Broadcast.** For a flake system, broadcast flake into the wet base coat, let it cure, then scrape and vacuum the excess.
7. **Topcoat.** Apply the clear topcoat, with anti-slip additive where you want it.
8. **Cure and walkthrough.** We walk the floor with you and give you the dates for foot traffic and parking in writing.

Timeline: most garages run about 1 to 3 days of work, then cure time before you park.

## Moisture vapor testing

Concrete is porous, and slabs, especially basement floors and older garage slabs without a vapor barrier under them, push moisture vapor up from the ground. Trap that vapor under a coating and it builds pressure, which shows up as bubbles, blisters or peeling.

We test before we pick a system. Common methods include a plastic-sheet test as a quick screen, calcium chloride tests that measure the rate of vapor coming out of the slab, and relative humidity probes set into the concrete. If readings are high, the system gets a moisture-mitigating primer rated for those conditions. Basements with radon mitigation systems need the suction point and any slab penetrations kept sealed and accessible, and the coating works around them.

## Cure temperatures in Massachusetts shoulder seasons

Coatings are chemistry, and chemistry is temperature-sensitive. Most epoxy products want the slab and air around 50°F or warmer during application and cure, and the slab needs to stay a few degrees above the dew point so condensation doesn't form on it. Many polyaspartic products are rated for cooler temperatures, but every product has its own limits on the data sheet.

That matters in late fall and early spring. A garage in Holden or Westford can be 60°F at noon and near freezing overnight, and a cold slab on a humid spring morning can sweat. We check the slab temperature and dew point, schedule around the forecast, and may use temporary heat for an unheated garage. Summer and early fall are the easiest windows; mid-winter work in an unheated garage usually waits.

## Road salt and freeze-thaw

Rock salt, calcium chloride and magnesium chloride brine are hard on bare concrete. They soak in, go through freeze-thaw cycles and cause surface flaking and spalling. It's common in the 1960s ranches and split-levels around Leominster and Fitchburg, and in the detached garages on bigger lots in Sterling, Bolton and Harvard.

A coating that is properly adhered to ground concrete seals the surface so salt water sits on top where you can squeegee or hose it out. Floors still need rinsing a few times a winter; salt residue left sitting dulls any topcoat over time.

## What to ask any flooring contractor

- Do you diamond grind or acid etch?
- Will you test the slab for moisture before choosing a system?
- What's the base coat, what's the topcoat, and how thick is the system?
- How are cracks, spalls and control joints handled?
- What slab temperature and dew point do you need to install, and what happens if the weather doesn't cooperate?
- When can I walk on it, and when can I park?
- Which exact products are you using? Manufacturer warranties vary by product, so get the names in writing.
