What a General Contractor Actually Coordinates on a Lancaster Job

Most people picture a general contractor as the person swinging the hammer. On a real remodel the job is closer to air-traffic control. A kitchen in Lancaster touches demolition, framing, rough plumbing, rough electrical, insulation, drywall, tile, cabinets, counters, trim, paint and final hookups. Each of those has to happen in a set order, each depends on the one before it, and several of them cannot legally proceed until an inspector has looked at the work. Our job is to line all of that up so the plumber is not standing in a kitchen that has not been demoed yet and the tile is not going down over a drain that still has to move.

Our own crew handles the carpentry, tile, flooring, paint and finish work. Plumbers and electricians come in for the rough-in and the final connections, and each of those trades pulls its own trade permit and stands in front of the inspector for its own work. We hire them, schedule them, check their work against the plan, and take responsibility for the gap between them. That gap is where a self-managed job usually falls apart: the framer finishes on a Wednesday, the electrician cannot come until the following week, and nobody owns the days in between.

Sequencing is the part homeowners never see. Cabinets have a lead time, so they are ordered before demolition starts, not after. Tile and plumbing fixtures are on site before the rough inspection so the plumber can set the valve at the right depth for the finished wall. Paint colors are picked before the trim goes up. None of that is complicated on its own, but there are dozens of those dependencies on an ordinary job, and missing one costs a week. We keep the list. You get the result.

Every job starts the same way, on paper. A written estimate that itemises scope, materials and exclusions, then a written contract before any work begins. Ask us for our registration and insurance details in writing and we will provide them directly — we do not publish those numbers online.

Lancaster Housing Stock — and How It Shapes the Plan

Lancaster is an old town, and the houses show it. Around the center and along the older roads you find antique farmhouses and colonials with post-and-beam or rough-sawn framing, fieldstone or brick foundations, plaster-and-lath walls, and floor systems that were sized by the carpenter's eye rather than a span table. Elsewhere there are capes and ranches from the middle of the last century, split-levels and colonials from the decades after that, and newer subdivision houses on larger lots. Each of those eras builds differently, and each one changes how we plan the job.

In an antique house, nothing is level, plumb or square, and we plan for that up front rather than discovering it with a cabinet run already on site. Plaster comes off in sheets, old wiring and iron drain lines are common behind the walls, and a rotted sill or a notched joist is not a surprise so much as a likelihood. That is why the written scope for an older Lancaster house carries clear allowances for what we expect to find and a change-order process for what we do not. Structural and rot repair is a regular part of our work in these houses — sills, rim joists, porch framing and the framing below a bathroom that has been leaking for years — and it is far cheaper to correct while the wall is already open than to come back later.

Mid-century capes and ranches are simpler to open up, but they have their own patterns: shallow eaves that ice up in winter, original bathrooms with tile set in a mortar bed, undersized bath fans that vent into the attic, and kitchens that were never designed for the appliances people own now. Newer colonials are the easiest to work in structurally, and they are reaching the age where builder-grade kitchens, baths and decks are wearing out at the same time. Knowing which of these you own tells us a lot about the permit, the schedule and the allowances before we ever pull a tape.

How Permitting Works Through the Lancaster Building Department

Any Lancaster project that alters structure, changes a room's use, adds a deck, or involves plumbing, gas or electrical work needs a permit from the town. The process is straightforward when it is run properly, and we run it for you. Here is what it looks like in general terms — the Building Department sets its own forms, fees and review times, and we confirm the current requirements with them at the start of every job rather than assuming.

Application. We prepare the building permit application with a description of the work, drawings where the scope calls for them, and the contractor information the town requires. For structural work such as removing a bearing wall or building a deck, the application includes the framing details the inspector needs to check the design against the state building code. The plumbing, gas and electrical contractors file their own trade permits for their portions of the job.

Review and issue. The Building Department reviews the application, may ask for clarification or additional detail, and issues the permit once it is satisfied. The permit is posted at the house. On a project that touches a septic system, a wetland buffer or a historic property, other town boards may have a say before the building permit is issued; we identify that early because it affects the schedule more than anything else.

Inspections. The permit is not a one-time signature. The inspector comes out at defined stages — typically after framing and rough plumbing and electrical are complete but before insulation and drywall cover them, and again at the end. Deck footings are inspected before concrete is poured. We schedule each inspection, make sure the work is ready and accessible, and are on site when the inspector arrives so questions get answered on the spot instead of becoming a failed inspection and a return visit.

Close-out. When the final inspection passes, the permit is closed. That closed permit is part of your house's record, and it matters when you refinance or sell. A job done without a permit, or with a permit that was pulled and never closed, is a problem that surfaces at the worst possible time. You should never be asked to pull a permit as the homeowner for work a contractor is doing; when the contractor holds the permit, the contractor holds the responsibility.

What a Written Scope Contains

The estimate is not a number on a business card. It is a document that describes the job precisely enough that two people reading it would build the same thing. If a line item is vague, the disagreement is already built in. These are the sections every MCB scope carries, and what each one is there to settle.

How Change Orders Are Handled

A change order is any addition, removal or substitution to the scope after the contract is signed. They come from two places. One is you: you see the kitchen open and decide the wall to the dining room should come down too, or you want the shower niche moved, or the paint color changes after the first coat. The other is the house: we open a wall and find a rotted sill, a chimney that was never lined, wiring that has to be replaced before the electrician will connect to it, or a floor that drops two inches across the room.

Either way, the handling is the same. We stop, we show you what we found or discuss what you want, and we write it up: what changes, what it costs or credits, and what it does to the schedule. You sign it, and only then does the work proceed. A change order is never discovered on the final invoice, and it is never done on a handshake and sorted out later. If a hidden condition is a safety issue that cannot wait, we make it safe first and then write the change order the same day.

On an older Lancaster house we tell you in the estimate phase which hidden conditions are likely, and we recommend you hold a contingency for them. A change order on a farmhouse is not a sign the job is going badly. It is the house telling you what it needs, and the process makes sure you decide how to answer it with the price in front of you.

What to Expect Week to Week

Every job is different in length, and the schedule in your contract is the one that counts. But the shape of a remodel is the same nearly every time, and knowing the shape makes the middle weeks a lot less stressful.

1. Protection, demolition and discovery

Floors and adjacent rooms are covered, dust barriers go up, and the old work comes out. This is loud and it is fast. It is also when the house tells us what it has been hiding, so it is the most likely week for a change order. You will hear from us the same day if we find something.

2. Framing and rough-in

Any structural repair, new walls, headers and subfloor go in, then the plumber and electrician run their rough work. The room looks worse than it did before demolition, which is normal. Fixture and cabinet locations are locked in during this phase, so this is the last cheap moment to change your mind about the layout.

3. Inspections and close-up

The rough inspections happen, insulation goes in, and drywall or plaster repair closes the walls. There can be a quiet day or two while we wait for an inspection slot; it is built into the schedule, not a sign the crew has left. Once the walls are closed the room starts to look like a room again.

4. Finishes

Tile, cabinets, counters, flooring, trim, doors and paint, in an order that keeps finished surfaces from being damaged by the next trade. Counters are usually templated after the cabinets are set and fabricated off site, which is why there is often a gap between cabinets going in and the sink working. Final plumbing and electrical connections come at the end of this phase.

5. Punch list, final inspection and close-out

We walk the job with you, write down every item that is not right, and fix the list. The final inspection is scheduled and the permit is closed. Debris is hauled, the space is cleaned, and the final payment is due after you have signed off on the walkthrough, not before.

Through all of it, you have one person to call. That person knows the schedule, the subs, the material orders and what is happening tomorrow. We broom-sweep daily and we tell you when a day will be loud, dusty or without water. Office consultations at our Lancaster office on Main Street are by appointment; most first meetings still happen at your kitchen table, where the project actually is.

Running a Lancaster Project — FAQ

Who pulls the permit on a Lancaster project, me or the contractor?

The contractor. MCB prepares and files the building permit application with the Lancaster Building Department, and the plumbing, gas and electrical contractors file their own trade permits for their work. We schedule every inspection and close the permit when the final passes. If a contractor asks you to pull the permit in your own name, that is a warning sign: the permit holder is the party responsible for the work.

What inspections happen during a remodel?

It depends on the scope, but a typical kitchen or bath has rough inspections for framing, plumbing and electrical before the walls are closed, then final inspections at the end. A deck adds a footing inspection before concrete is poured. The Building Department sets the exact stages for your permit, and we build each one into the schedule so the crew is not waiting on an inspector unexpectedly.

What happens if you find rot or old wiring after the walls are open?

We stop, show you, and write a change order that describes the repair, its cost and its effect on the schedule. You approve it before the work continues. If the condition is a safety issue we make it safe first and paper it the same day. On older Lancaster houses we tell you in the estimate which of these conditions are likely so you can plan a contingency.

How do allowances work in the written scope?

An allowance is a set dollar amount in the contract for an item you have not chosen yet, such as tile or plumbing fixtures. When you make the selection, the difference between the allowance and the actual price is credited or added through a change order. It lets the contract be signed before every finish is picked without hiding the cost of your choices.

Do I need to be home while the work is going on?

No. Most of our clients are at work during the day. We agree on access, alarm codes and which rooms are off limits before we start, and you get a heads-up before any day that will be loud, dusty or without water or power. Decisions that need you are flagged in advance so they do not hold up the crew.

Does your process change for an antique farmhouse versus a newer colonial?

The steps are the same; the allowances and the planning are different. In an antique house we expect out-of-level floors, plaster walls, old plumbing and possible sill or joist repair, and the scope says so. In a newer house the structure is predictable and the schedule is usually tighter. Either way you get a written scope, a written contract and a permit in the contractor's name.
