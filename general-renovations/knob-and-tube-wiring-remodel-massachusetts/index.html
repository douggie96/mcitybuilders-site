Knob-and-Tube Wiring in an Older Massachusetts Home: What It Costs to Deal With During a Remodel

Last reviewed 2026-10-16

If your remodel turns up knob-and-tube wiring, plan on replacing the circuits in the rooms you are opening, and budget roughly $3,000–$9,000 for that rewire inside the remodel, plus $2,500–$7,000 if the house also needs a 200-amp service upgrade. Doing it while the walls are already open is the cheapest moment you will ever get. The electrician pulls the electrical permit, the work gets inspected, and then the walls close up.

What knob-and-tube is, and why Central MA has so much of it

Knob-and-tube is the wiring method used in most American houses built from roughly the 1890s into the 1930s and early 1940s. Single conductors run separately through ceramic tubes in the framing and along ceramic knobs, with air around each wire to shed heat. There is no ground conductor.

Central Massachusetts is full of houses from that era. Mill-town triple-deckers in Clinton, Worcester and Fitchburg, and antique colonials and farmhouses in Harvard and Lancaster, often still have original circuits live. A house can look updated at the panel and still have knob-and-tube feeding the upstairs bedrooms.

Why it becomes a problem the moment you remodel

Knob-and-tube that has been left alone for 90 years is not automatically on fire. The trouble is what a remodel does to it:

  • No ground. Modern kitchens and baths need grounded and GFCI-protected outlets. You cannot properly tie new kitchen or bath circuits into ungrounded wiring.
  • Insulation buries it. The system depends on open air to cool the wires. The National Electrical Code (Section 394.12) says concealed knob-and-tube shall not be used in hollow spaces that are insulated with loose, blown-in, rolled-in or foamed-in insulation that envelops the conductors. Massachusetts adopts the NEC with state amendments as the Massachusetts Electrical Code, 527 CMR 12.00. Mass Save also treats live knob-and-tube as a barrier that has to be dealt with before insulation work goes in.
  • Brittle insulation and buried splices. A century of heat, rodents and past add-ons leaves cloth-and-rubber insulation cracked and splices hidden.
  • Insurance carriers. Many home insurance carriers will not write a new policy on a house with active knob-and-tube, or charge more until it is replaced. Not universal, but common.

Why open walls are the cheapest moment to replace it

Most of the cost of a rewire is access: getting new cable through finished plaster without tearing the house apart. Once a kitchen or bathroom is open to the studs, new grounded circuits go in without the fishing and cutting.

So we plan for it up front. On a kitchen or whole-floor renovation, we would rather know about knob-and-tube at the walkthrough than find it after cabinets are ordered. Our guide to surprises in older Central MA kitchen remodels covers the other things that tend to show up alongside it.

What it costs during a remodel

Our typical ranges for work coordinated inside a remodel. Your number depends on how many circuits are still live and how much of the house is open.

Item Typical range
Knob-and-tube rewire (scope inside a remodel) $3,000–$9,000
200-amp service upgrade $2,500–$7,000
Load-bearing wall removal with beam (if the plan opens up rooms) $10,000–$25,000
Galvanized supply line replacement (pre-1960 homes) $800–$2,500

Older houses rarely hide just one thing. Knob-and-tube and galvanized water lines often travel together, which is why our bathroom remodel cost guide for Massachusetts builds in room for hidden conditions.

The 60-amp or 100-amp service question. Many pre-war houses still run on a 60-amp or 100-amp service. A new kitchen plus a heat pump can push past what that service carries. The electrician runs a load calculation, and if the answer is 200 amps, doing it in the same project means the utility coordination, meter work and new panel happen once instead of twice.

Permits, inspection, and who does what

The electrician pulls the electrical permit with your town and calls the wiring inspector for rough and final inspections. Maverick City Builders is the general contractor: we schedule the electrician, sequence demo and framing around their rough-in, and handle the building permit side. We do not do the electrical work ourselves. See our overview of building permits in Central Massachusetts for how the different permits fit together.

How it is sequenced inside a kitchen or bath remodel, and the timeline

A typical sequence looks like this:

  1. Walkthrough and assessment. The electrician confirms which circuits are still knob-and-tube and whether the service needs upgrading.
  2. Lead-safe demo. Plaster in pre-1978 homes is treated as containing lead paint. We hold the Massachusetts lead-safe renovation credential and follow the state programme’s containment and cleanup practices when opening walls.
  3. Electrical rough-in. New grounded circuits go in; old runs in the work area are disconnected and abandoned or removed. Inside an open room this goes quickly; the size of the scope sets the days.
  4. Rough inspection. The wiring inspector signs off before anything is closed up.
  5. Fishing beyond the remodel. Circuits in finished rooms are fished through small openings. Expect a few days of plaster patching afterward.
  6. Insulation, drywall, finishes. Only after inspection.

The added time comes mostly from the inspector’s schedule in your town and plaster patching outside the remodel area, so we build both into the schedule at the start. For matching patched plaster and old trim, see our notes on painting older homes in Massachusetts.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to replace knob-and-tube wiring in Massachusetts?

When it is done as part of a kitchen, bath or whole-floor remodel, a knob-and-tube rewire typically runs $3,000 to $9,000, depending on how many circuits are still live and how much of the house is already open. If the electrical service also needs to go to 200 amps, add roughly $2,500 to $7,000. Rewiring a fully finished house without a remodel usually costs more because of plaster access.

Can you put insulation over knob-and-tube wiring?

Not safely, and the National Electrical Code says concealed knob-and-tube shall not be used in hollow spaces insulated with loose, blown-in, rolled-in or foamed-in insulation that envelops the wires. Knob-and-tube relies on open air to shed heat. In Massachusetts, Mass Save treats live knob-and-tube as a barrier that must be addressed before insulation upgrades. Have an electrician evaluate and replace it first.

Will my homeowners insurance cover a house with knob-and-tube wiring?

It depends on the carrier. Many home insurance carriers decline new policies on houses with active knob-and-tube wiring, charge higher premiums, or require an electrician's evaluation. It is not universal, but it is common. Replacing the circuits during a remodel and keeping the electrical permit and inspection paperwork usually makes the conversation with insurance carriers much easier.

Do I need a permit to replace knob-and-tube wiring in Massachusetts?

Yes. Rewiring is electrical work, so the electrician pulls an electrical permit with your town and the local wiring inspector checks the work at rough-in and final. If the rewire is part of a larger remodel, the general contractor typically handles the building permit for the rest of the job and schedules around the electrical inspections.

Is it worth replacing knob-and-tube wiring during a kitchen remodel?

Yes, in almost every case. Once the kitchen is open to the studs, the electrician can run new grounded circuits quickly, without the cutting and patching a standalone rewire requires. New kitchens need grounded, GFCI-protected outlets and dedicated appliance circuits that knob-and-tube cannot provide. Waiting means opening finished walls again later, at higher cost.
