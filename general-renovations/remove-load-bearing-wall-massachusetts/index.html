Removing a Load-Bearing Wall in Massachusetts: Cost, Permit and Timeline

Last reviewed 2026-10-16

Removing a load-bearing wall in Massachusetts typically costs $10,000–$25,000 with Maverick City Builders, including the new beam, temporary shoring, posts carried down to the foundation and patching. You need a building permit with an engineered beam specification, and the contractor pulls it. Plan on a few weeks for engineering and the permit, then roughly one to two weeks on site plus finish work.

Why so many Central MA homeowners want this wall gone

The most common open-concept request we hear is the same house over and over: a 1980s or 1990s colonial with a closed-in kitchen on one side and a formal dining room nobody uses on the other. A typical colonial in Westford or Shrewsbury has a wall between those two rooms that also holds up the floor above. Older capes and antique post-and-beam homes around Lancaster and Hudson raise the same question. If your goal is a bigger kitchen, our guide to opening up a closed kitchen in an older home walks through the layout side.

How to tell if a wall is load-bearing

There are clues you can check yourself:

  • Joist direction. Go to the basement or attic. If the floor or ceiling joists run perpendicular to the wall and land on it, the wall is likely carrying them.
  • A beam or girder below. A wall sitting directly over the main girder or a steel beam in the basement is a strong sign it is bearing.
  • Walls stacked above. If a second-floor wall sits right on top of it, that load usually comes down through this wall.

These are clues, not answers. Only a contractor opening the framing, and an engineer sizing the beam, can confirm it.

What the work actually involves

The load the wall carried has to go somewhere at every step.

  1. Engineering. A structural engineer sizes the new beam, usually an LVL (laminated veneer lumber) or a steel beam, and specifies posts and footings.
  2. Flush or dropped beam. A dropped beam hangs below the ceiling and is simpler. A flush beam sits up in the joist space for a clean, flat ceiling, but the joists must be cut and hung from it, which adds labour.
  3. Temporary shoring. We build temporary walls on both sides to hold the floor above while the old wall comes out.
  4. Point loads to the foundation. The beam ends put concentrated load on posts. Those loads must carry all the way down, which can mean new posts or footings in the basement.
  5. Moving what is inside the wall. Electrical circuits, heating ducts or pipes, and sometimes a drain line often run through it. The electrician and a Massachusetts master plumber relocate those.
  6. Inspection, then finish. Once the framing passes inspection, we patch the ceiling and walls, then tie in the floor where the wall stood. With hardwood, that usually means weaving new boards into the old so the patch disappears; see our hardwood floor installation page.

When the framing itself is compromised, that becomes structural repair work and we handle it as part of the same job.

Old-house surprises to plan for

Older homes hide things until the wall is open. Plaster and lath is messier than drywall and harder to patch. Knob-and-tube wiring turns up in antique houses and should not be buried again. Undersized or notched framing from past renovations has to be corrected before the beam goes in.

If the house was built before 1978, assume lead paint until proven otherwise. We hold the Massachusetts lead-safe renovation credential under the state programme and run the job with containment, dust control and proper cleanup.

Permits: what Massachusetts requires

Structural work like this needs a building permit under the Massachusetts State Building Code (780 CMR), which includes the residential code. Your town’s building department will want the engineered beam specification with the application, and an inspector checks the framing before it is closed up. The contractor pulls the permit, not the homeowner. Your town sets the fee. Our page on building permits in Central Massachusetts explains how the process runs locally.

What it costs

Scope Typical MCB range
Non-load-bearing wall removal $4,000–$9,000
Load-bearing wall removal with beam $10,000–$25,000
200-amp electrical service upgrade (if needed) $2,500–$7,000
Knob-and-tube rewire (if found) $3,000–$9,000

Span length, LVL versus steel, flush versus dropped, new footings, and how much plumbing, ductwork, flooring and plaster is involved all move the price. The electrical items apply only if we find them, and we tell you before any extra work starts.

How long it takes

Typical ranges, not promises:

  • Engineering and permit: typically a few weeks, depending on the engineer’s schedule and your town’s review time.
  • On-site structural work: for a typical bearing-wall removal, about one to two weeks, including shoring, beam, posts, utilities and inspection.
  • Finish work: drywall or plaster, paint and floor tie-in follow.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to remove a load-bearing wall in Massachusetts?

With Maverick City Builders, removing a load-bearing wall with a new beam typically runs $10,000–$25,000. The price depends on span length, LVL or steel, flush or dropped beam, posts and footings, and how much electrical, plumbing or ductwork must move. A non-load-bearing wall is typically $4,000–$9,000. Surprises like knob-and-tube wiring are priced separately and discussed before work starts.

Do I need a permit to remove a load-bearing wall in MA?

Yes. Removing a bearing wall is structural work and needs a building permit under the Massachusetts State Building Code (780 CMR). The building department normally wants an engineered beam specification with the application, and an inspector checks the framing before it is covered. The contractor pulls the permit, and your town sets the fee.

How can I tell if a wall is load-bearing?

Look for joists running perpendicular to the wall and resting on it, a beam or girder directly below it in the basement, or another wall stacked on top of it upstairs. Centre walls in colonials are often bearing. These are only clues, because framing can surprise you. A contractor opening the wall and a structural engineer sizing the beam are the only real confirmation.

How long does it take to remove a load-bearing wall?

Engineering and the building permit typically take a few weeks. On site, a typical bearing-wall removal takes about one to two weeks for shoring, demolition, the new beam, posts, moving utilities and inspection. Drywall or plaster repair, painting and the floor tie-in come after. Older homes and bigger spans can add time, so treat these as typical ranges, not guarantees.

Is an LVL or a steel beam better for wall removal?

Neither is better in every case; the engineer picks based on the load and span. LVL beams are wood, lighter to handle and common for typical residential spans. Steel carries more load in a shallower depth, which helps with long openings or flush beams under heavy loads, but costs more and is harder to install. Either way, the posts below must carry the load to the foundation.
